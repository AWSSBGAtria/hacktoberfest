Judging & Mentors Handbook Start here
For working professionals

Start here.

You are joining a one-day, in-person open-source hack day for college students — part of the global Hacktoberfest celebration, run with Major League Hacking. Expect a room of 100+ student builders, many of them contributing to open source for the first time, building for seven hours straight. Your presence — a question answered, a Git command unblocked, a fair score sheet — is the difference between a project that ships and one that stalls at 2 PM.

What it is

A free, full-day Hacktoberfest Hack Day: students build open-source AI projects in solo teams or teams of up to four, then demo for prizes.

Who is here

100+ college students, from first-year beginners to seasoned contributors, plus mentors, judges, volunteers, and faculty.

Your part

Mentors keep builders unblocked all day. Judges score the pitches in a single hour at 5:30 PM. Many of you will do both.

Why this day matters Two minutes of context
  • Hacktoberfest is DigitalOcean's month-long global celebration of open source. A Hack Day is its in-person form: one room, one day, real pull requests.
  • Many builders are beginners. Their first-ever commit, branch, or pull request may happen at this event — often with you standing there. Patience tends to help more than speed; the technical answers are usually easy by comparison.
  • Come as a person, not a brand. A light mention of where you work is natural; what students need is genuine curiosity about what they are building. The MLH code of conduct applies to everyone — including us.
  • What students take home. Beyond the swag, it is a fair, well-run judging round and a thoughtful conversation with someone who does this for a living — that is the part they remember.
Draft — confirm with organizers

Judge and mentor headcounts, exact check-in instructions for industry guests, and whether pros receive food coupons are to be confirmed. If your name is on the roster, you will get a final briefing note closer to the day.

Judging & Mentors Handbook Field guide
Shared vocabulary

Hackathon field guide.

The words you will hear on the floor today, in plain language. If a student uses one of these and you draw a blank, this page has your back — no one expects you to memorize it.

Hack Day
A one-day, in-person hackathon. Teams build a working project from scratch in a few hours, then demo it.
Hacktoberfest
DigitalOcean's annual global open-source celebration. This event is an official Hacktoberfest Hack Day.
MLH
Major League Hacking — the student hackathon league partnering this event. Registration, submissions, and the code of conduct run through MLH.
Repo
A project's repository on GitHub — its code, history, and home. Every submission must live in a public repo.
Issue
A task or bug logged on a repo. "Good first issues" are beginner-friendly tasks mentors help teams find.
Pull request (PR)
A proposed change to a repo, sent for review. The core currency of open source — and of Hacktoberfest.
Open-source license
The legal terms that let others use and build on code (MIT, Apache-2.0, GPL...). Required for the core track.
Open-weight model
An AI model whose weights are publicly available — like Gemma. Distinct from open-source, though often used together.
Agent skill (SKILL.md)
A capability package for AI agents, written to the open Agent Skill Standard — typically a SKILL.md file plus supporting resources.
Model harness
Software that runs and orchestrates a model — loading it, feeding it input, handling output. Original harnesses are a valid core-track entry.
Gemma 4 / Gemini API
Google's open-weight multimodal model, accessed for this event through the Gemini API or hosted locally. The basis of the Gemma 4 track.
Track
The challenge category a project competes in. Three today — and while one project can qualify for more than one, teams submit and are judged in exactly one.
Submission
The team's entry on the MLH challenges page: repo link, write-up, demo notes. Opens 5:00 PM; locks at 5:30 PM — and it names the single track the team is judged in.
Pitch
The three minutes a team has to show what they built, followed by one to two minutes of questions. A volunteer at the table keeps the time.
Judging workbook
The shared scorecard judges work in on the day: six criteria, scores 0–10, everything else calculated. Access opens at 5:00 PM — thirty minutes before judging.
DeepAgentLabs
Sponsor and governing partner of the Best Open-Source AI Project track — open-source tools for operating agentic AI. Featured projects are showcased on deepagentlabs.io.
Great Data Labs
Sponsor of the Best Use of Gemma 4 track. Their open-source great-generator makes synthetic datasets — optional to use, with plus points when paired with locally hosted Gemma 4.

Judging & Mentors Handbook Your role
Mentors & judges

Your role.

Two hats, one room. Mentors keep teams moving all day; judges evaluate the final pitches in one focused hour. Most industry guests wear both — mentor in the day, judge in the evening. If you are only judging, arriving at 5:00 PM is right: that is when your judging workbook access opens.

On the floor

Mentor

10:15 AM – 5:00 PM · roaming

You circulate the room, answer questions, unblock stuck teams, and give feedback on ideas before they become 4 PM regrets. You are a guide, not a pair-programmer — the code stays with the students.

  • Unblock, then step back. Get them past the wall, then let them drive again.
  • Questions you will hear: Git commands, finding good first issues, "does this idea fit a track?", API keys, where to start with Gemma or AWS.
  • Roam rather than nest. Split the room with fellow mentors so no table goes unanswered for long.
  • If something serious comes up — conduct, safety, a dispute — bring it to the organizer desk and let us take it from there.
At the desk

Judge

5:30 PM – 6:30 PM · judging stations

You watch each team's three-minute pitch, take one to two minutes of questions — a volunteer at your table keeps the time — then score six criteria in the judging workbook. Your access opens at 5:00 PM; the 5:15 PM briefing walks you through it before the first team arrives.

  • Submissions close at 5:30 PM. Only submitted projects can be judged.
  • Score, don't coach. Mentoring ends when judging begins.
  • One project, one track. A project may qualify for several tracks, but it is submitted and judged in exactly one — no switching during judging; flag requests to an organizer.
  • Flag conflicts — teams you mentored closely, your own organization, or anyone you know personally.
One standard, all day

MLH's code of conduct applies to every minute you are at this event, in every interaction. Please be kind, be inclusive, keep it professional. Worth a read before you arrive: static.mlh.io/docs/mlh-code-of-conduct.pdf. If anything ever concerns you, the lead organizer is on page 12 — we would rather hear from you early than not at all.

Judging & Mentors Handbook Track 1 · Open-Source AI
Track 1 of 3 · Core Hacktoberfest Challenge

Best open-source AI project.

The core Hacktoberfest challenge: open-source AI doing real work — a new project, or a meaningful extension, integration, evaluation, policy pack or operational feature. DeepAgentLabs governs this track and showcases featured entries on deepagentlabs.io. Scores come from the same six criteria as every other track.

Track sponsor & governing partner

DeepAgentLabs

Open tools for operating agentic AI — observability, runtime supervision, evaluation, reliability testing and MCP connectivity. Six PyPI packages, ~402K+ downloads, 10+ contributors; projects using them are featured on the site.

deepagentlabs.io github.com/DeepAgentLabs
What teams are building Eligibility gate
  • Open-source or open-weight AI is an important part of the project — a new project or a meaningful extension, integration, example, evaluation, policy pack or operational feature.
  • A public GitHub repo under an open-source license. Using DeepAgentLabs packages is one path, not a requirement — stand-alone open-source work qualifies, and strong entries get showcased on deepagentlabs.io.
  • An agent skill must comply with the Agent Skill Open Standard (SKILL.md).
  • A model-harness entry needs an original implementation, or meaningful changes to an existing open-source harness.
Track focus Shapes the six criteria — no separate weight
What judges look for What good looks like
Meaningful open-source AI use The core capability comes from openly licensed, self-hostable models or tooling — not a proprietary API behind a curtain.
Technical depth & implementation Architecture, training and inference pipelines, evaluation rigour, reproducibility — evidence the hard problems were actually solved.
Quality of the contribution A repo a stranger can pick up tomorrow: commit and issue hygiene, reusable modules, real value for the community.
Docs, structure & licence README, setup instructions, architecture diagrams, model and dataset cards, a correct LICENSE and third-party attribution.
Clear demonstration A judge can run it — or watch it run — and reproduce the claimed result from the documented steps alone.
Before you score this track

Start with the repo: confirm it is public and carries a real license file, then judge the build as presented today — not the idea it might become.

Judging & Mentors Handbook Track 2 · Gemma 4
Track 2 of 3 · Sponsored challenge

Best use of Gemma 4.

Build with Gemma 4 — Google's open-weight multimodal model — through the Gemini API or hosted locally: text and images together, a focused tool, or a fast prototype. Sponsored by Great Data Labs, whose open-source great-generator teams may use — optional, with plus points when paired with locally hosted Gemma 4.

Track sponsor

Great Data Labs

Practical tools for modern data and AI engineering. Their flagship project, great-generator, is a Python library for schema-driven synthetic data — relational datasets, Pandas and Spark workflows, CSV/Parquet/Delta output. Use it or not; pair it with locally hosted Gemma 4 for the plus points.

github.com/ravikiranpagidi/great-generator greatdatalabs@proton.me
What teams are building Challenge framing
  • Multimodal experiences — understanding text and images together, where the problem calls for it.
  • Focused AI tools — for learning, creativity, productivity, or community.
  • Rapid prototyping — idea to working prototype, quickly, through the Gemini API or a local model.
Track focus Shapes the six criteria — no separate weight
What judges look for What good looks like
Effective, meaningful Gemma 4 use The model does real work at the heart of the product — you can point at exactly where Gemma 4 changes the outcome, rather than it being bolted on.
Multimodal where relevant Genuine text-and-image use where the problem needs it; for text-only projects, judge the depth of prompting or tuning instead.
Quality of AI integration Prompt design, context handling, fine-tuning, evals, guardrails, latency and cost, fallbacks — output validated before it is trusted.
Originality A fresh application of the model, not a wrapper around the obvious demo — novel task framing, clever data pipelines, unexpected use-cases.
The demo shows the model's work Gemma 4's contribution is self-evident in the pitch — an A/B against a no-AI baseline is the strongest possible proof.
Two questions worth asking

"Where does Gemma 4 actually change the result — and not just any LLM?" and, when the team used great-generator, "was the model hosted locally, and what did the data pipeline look like?"

Judging & Mentors Handbook Track 3 · Best Use of AWS
Track 3 of 3 · AWS Student Builder Group Special

Best use of AWS.

Use the AWS ecosystem to turn an idea into something real and scalable. Any AWS service counts and combining several is encouraged — but AWS must be an important part of the project, with a clear explanation of how it contributes. This track scores four specific things on top of the six shared criteria.

Host & track owner

AWS Student Builder Group

Run by our own AWS Student Builder Group at Atria — the club hosting the day. The strongest AWS-powered entry takes the track.

How this track is judged Four questions on top of the six criteria
01

Resource efficiency

How efficiently are the resources used — right-sized services, no idle waste, no over-provisioned "just in case".

02

Measured usage

Can the team show how they measured it? Real numbers — CloudWatch, cost dashboards, load tests — beat guesses.

03

Services together

How well do the services work together as one system — rather than several services sitting side by side.

04

Fit at scale

Are these the right services for this project at scale — what happens at ten times the users, without a rebuild?

Track focus Shapes the six criteria — no separate weight
What judges look for What good looks like
Meaningful, effective AWS use Beyond EC2-plus-a-bucket: services chosen because they are the right tool, with reasoning that holds up.
Architecture & implementation Clear diagrams, separation of concerns, IAM design, infrastructure as code, a deployment story anyone could reproduce.
Scalability, reliability, cost Well-Architected thinking: autoscaling, observability, cost-awareness, graceful failure handling — this is where efficiency and fit at scale show up.
Creative combinations Unexpected but sensible pairings — Bedrock with Step Functions and EventBridge, serverless pipelines, event-driven choreography.
The demo traces the cloud Follow the request through the real architecture, ideally with CloudWatch logs, traces or metrics shown live.
Before you score this track

"It runs on my laptop, but it could be on AWS" does not clear the bar — and when you ask what they measured, the numbers should be real.

Judging & Mentors Handbook How judging works
One hour, six criteria

How judging works.

Your judging workbook opens at 5:00 PM. Each team gets three minutes to pitch and one to two of questions — timed by a volunteer at your table. Then you score six criteria, 0–10 each; the weighted total calculates itself. Not submitted by 5:30? Not evaluated.

Step 01

Briefing

5:15 PM — criteria, pitch format, and a walk through the workbook. Questions here, not at the tables.

Step 02

Pitch & questions

Three minutes, then one to two — the volunteer keeps the time. Then the next team.

Step 03

Scores in

All six filed before 6:30. A team's score is the average of every judge on it.

The six criteria Weights from the judging workbook
Criterion Weight What a strong answer looks like
Technical implementation 25% Architecture does what it claims; the hard parts are genuinely solved.
Innovation & creativity 20% A fresh angle on the problem, not a re-skinned tutorial.
Problem & impact 15% A real problem, for real people, with a path to matter.
Execution & functionality 15% It works in front of you — or fails honestly, with a reason why.
Presentation & demo 15% Clear story, confident demo, questions handled, impact clear.
Documentation & code quality 10% A stranger could clone it, run it and understand it.
The 0–10 scale Whole numbers · all six required
9–10

Outstanding — does everything at a high level.

7–8

Strong and complete.

5–6

Solid but unremarkable.

3–4

Major gaps.

0–2

Barely working.

Rules of the sheet Non-negotiables
  • One project, one track. A project may qualify for several, but it is submitted and judged in exactly one. Asked to switch mid-round? Note the team name and tell an organizer.
  • Score what is presented. Judge the build as it stands today, not the idea it might become — and file all six scores before closing the row.
  • Scores stay yours until filing. No score-talk with other judges beforehand; declare conflicts (teams you mentored, your organization, anyone you know) and hand that team over.
  • Amend, don't argue. To correct a score, fix the scorecard cell — the standings recalculate themselves. Disputes go to the lead organizer, not the team.

Judging & Mentors Handbook Mentoring
Seven hours on the floor

Mentoring on the floor.

Most of your day is conversations at tables: "how do I push this?", "is this a good idea?", "where do I even start?". You will not remember every answer you gave — the students will remember how it felt to ask.

Do
  • Start with the team, not the code. What are you building, and why this? Thirty seconds of context saves a wrong direction.
  • Teach the command, not just the fix. "Here is git rebase" beats typing it on their laptop for them.
  • Point at good first issues and beginner-safe repos when a team wants an open-source starting point.
  • Encourage scope cuts. A small thing that works beats a big thing that never runs. 4 PM is coming.
  • Check back once. A two-minute follow-up lap separates a good mentor from a great one.
  • Send food-and-water signals. If a table has not moved in hours, ask if they have eaten.
Don't
  • The code and the submission stay the students'. Writing it for them sits outside both the spirit of the day and the rules.
  • Let them keep the keyboard — even when someone is truly stuck, narrating a fix tends to teach more than typing it does.
  • Keep employer talk light. A mention of where you work is natural; a recruiting pitch at every table gets tiring fast.
  • Prizes, referrals and jobs are not ours to promise — and a promise students remember turns into one they chase.
  • The afternoon stall, 3–5 PM, tends to be when mentors are needed most — worth being around for.
  • Opinions before 5:30 are coaching; after 5:30 they are scores. When judging starts, mentoring stops.
If you only remember one thing The 3-minute rule
Listen for one minute

Let the team explain, even badly. Most "stuck" teams have already said the answer out loud — they just did not hear it.

Unblock for two

Give the smallest next step — a command, a doc link, a name to search. Not the full architecture of the finished project.

Then walk away

Hand the keyboard back. Come back later if they wave you down. Their hands should be on the keys more than yours.

Anything serious goes up, not out

Conduct concerns, safety issues, harassment, or disputes about rules: stop, find the organizer desk, hand it over. Do not improvise a resolution, and do not post about it publicly. The lead organizer's number is on page 12.
